Caseload & Capacity

How work is distributed, covered and forecast across utilization management and care management — and what a supervisor can actually change from the screen.

Three questions

A supervisor asks the same three things every morning

Who is carrying what. Who is actually here. What is coming. Almost every workforce screen in the product answers one of those three, and the ones that answer none were cut.

The distinction that organises all of it: work is either sitting unclaimed in a queue, or it belongs to one person — never both. A queue is the pool you pull from; the moment someone owns a case it is no longer pullable. Anything counted in both places is counted twice in every workload, utilization and queue-depth figure downstream, and the two halves of the dashboard stop reconciling.

Queued or owned, enforced in the data

Assigning a case pulls it out of its queue. Returning one to a queue releases whoever held it. Both moves go through a single pair of functions rather than being re-derived at each screen, so the rule holds wherever a reassignment starts — the bulk panel, a row action, a drill-down, a balance run.

Rebalancing

Moving work, with the move on the record

Four strategies, chosen rather than automatic, each previewed before anything commits.

StrategyWhat it doesUsed when
LightMoves one item from the busiest personA single hotspot, mid-shift
StandardRebalances threeRoutine morning levelling
AggressiveRebalances sixAfter an absence or a volume spike
Even outLevels everyone toward the team averageStart of week, or after a roster change

Every strategy shows the plan first — who loses what, who gains it, and what each person's utilization becomes — and nothing moves until it is confirmed. Reassignment can also be done by hand from any caseload row or any drill-down, scoped to one person's work, with a recommended target marked and over-capacity targets flagged before you pick them.

Each move is written to assignment history with who moved it, from whom, to whom, and which members were affected. A rebalance that leaves no trace is indistinguishable from a caseload that drifted.

Who is actually here

Scheduled shift against worked shift, per person, with five exception types rather than a present/absent flag.

On time Late start Early leave Absence Overtime

Overtime sits in the same list as the shortfalls on purpose. A team hitting its numbers on sustained overtime is a staffing finding, not a success, and separating it into a different view is how it gets missed.

Adherence is reported by person and by exception type across daily, weekly, monthly and rolling four-week periods, alongside PTO balances — accrued, taken, scheduled, remaining — so cover can be planned against what people actually have left rather than against who volunteers.

Planned absence redistributes within the team

The PTO flow empties a person's caseload completely for the dates they are away, to teammates on their own team only — never across teams. Cross-team redistribution looks efficient on a utilization chart and is usually wrong: the receiving reviewer does not hold the clinical proficiency, the market, or the programme the work belongs to. Targets can be narrowed further by hand where only certain colleagues should take it.

What is coming

Eight weeks of actual submission and referral volume, bucketed from each item's own received date, with a projection for next week and a comparison against capacity.

The projection is a trailing four-complete-week average. The current week is shown on the trend line but excluded from the basis, because a partial week always reads as a collapse in volume and would drag every forecast down.

This is a planning heuristic, not a statistical forecast

It is deliberately simple, and it is labelled as simple on the screen. A supervisor deciding whether next week needs cover is well served by a trailing average against remaining capacity; presenting that as a model would invite a confidence it has not earned. Where the projection exceeds capacity the screen says so plainly and names the gap.

"Capacity" means two different things by scope, and the screen distinguishes them: overall it is intake-processing capacity; per team it is that team's remaining active-caseload headroom, since intake coordinators serve every team and have no per-team capacity of their own.

Escalation

When the answer is not redistribution

Some work does not need a different owner — it needs a different level of review.

  • A named Medical Director, for clinical questions a nurse reviewer cannot resolve
  • The peer-to-peer review queue, where the requesting provider needs a clinician conversation
  • The supervisor, for anything that is an operational rather than a clinical problem

Escalation is a distinct action from reassignment and lands in the same history. The distinction matters at review time: a case that went to a Medical Director because the criteria were genuinely ambiguous tells you something different from a case that was moved three times because nobody had capacity.

Next phase

Where this goes

Three things the current model does not do, stated because a workforce conversation that omits them is not worth having.

  • Skill-based routing beyond discipline. Work is matched to a team and a discipline today. Language, licensure by state, and member-specific continuity are not yet routing inputs — they are the ones a real assignment engine needs next.
  • Forecasting by seasonality. A trailing average cannot see an open-enrolment spike or a flu season coming. Doing that properly needs history the demonstration environment does not carry.
  • Cost of cover. Overtime is measured as an adherence exception, not priced. Connecting it to the financial view is a small piece of work and turns a staffing observation into a budget conversation.
