After

Widening the time range made the event count go down

audit-traceability.ts · shell.html

The module was borrowing the shared dashboard period bar, where 30 days is secretly the unfiltered baseline — every pending case falls inside it, so it stands in for "everything". That is fine on a caseload tab and wrong on a log that spans a decade: moving from 30 days to QTD narrowed the view from all events down to ninety days, and the count fell.

Before
  • Shared period bar: Today / 7 days / 30 days = everything / QTD
  • Widening the window reduced the result
  • Nothing stated how much history existed
After
  • Own Range control: Today / 7 / 30 / 90 days / 12 months / All history, defaulting to All
  • Every option means what it says
  • The bar states the real span outright — Online store: 3,824 events, 2026-04-04 → 2026-07-17, with the archived count beside it

New

Retention & Archive

audit-trail.ts · audit-traceability.ts

The Audit Trail queries the online store, and the screen implied that was the whole retained record. It is not, and "where is year seven?" is the first question a records-retention reviewer asks.

Archived periods are represented by segment metadata — period, event count, hash range, storage tier, seal date, WORM lock, last verification, purge-eligible date, hold status — rather than by materialising a decade of individual events. That is how a real tiered store exposes cold data, and materialising them would misrepresent what is actually there.

The archive deliberately outruns the retention windowAn archive that stops at the ten-year boundary has no disposition queue and no segment a legal hold could matter to — the two things a records reviewer actually asks about. It reaches back to 2014-10-01 so that five segments sit past retention and two are held.
New

AI Oversight

src/app/data/ai-oversight.ts

Built because the agenda's second topic had no answer in the product. What existed was three hardcoded next-best-action cards carrying confidence numbers and a static distribution bar — nothing on how a score is produced, whether it can be trusted, or what happens when a clinician disagrees with it.

The dataset carries a real defect on purposeThe 95%+ band claims 97% and observes 83% — fourteen points hot across 121 of 247 determinations, which is what drags aggregate decision agreement to 87% against a 90% target. A model that is confidently wrong at the ceiling is the failure mode that actually causes harm, and a demo where every control passes persuades nobody.

Override reasons are split into model-attributable and legitimate clinical divergence, because an override rate on its own cannot be interpreted. Agreement by clinician is framed as a signal rather than a score, and nobody gets a rate below twenty scored determinations.

Fixed

Three generator bugs found while calibrating

src/app/data/ai-oversight.ts
Symptom
  • A confidence band read a flat 100% agreement
  • Override reasons only ever landed on two of six
  • The calibration ceiling permitted 100%
Cause and fix
  • Confidence and agreement were both permutations of the same two digits of the auth id, so each band drew a clustered sample instead of a scattered one. Both now hash over the full case identity.
  • Same clustered seed, same fix — and attribution now correlates with the over-confident band, which is where model-attributable overrides genuinely concentrate.
  • Capped below 100 deliberately: a band that never disagrees is a sign the measurement is broken, not that the model is perfect.

New

Certified disposition, and a hold that actually holds

src/app/shared/disposition.ts

A legal hold was a note saying disposition ought to be suspended. It is now a hard precondition: the disposal action refuses outright and names the hold that stopped it, regardless of the retention date. An eligible segment takes an independent countersignature and issues a certificate carrying the period, event count, retained terminal hash, retention basis, method and both signatories.

Why the terminal hash survivesOnce a segment is purged the events are gone. Unless something durable states what was destroyed and under whose authority, there is afterwards no evidence the record existed or that it was lawfully destroyed — and both halves matter. Closes REQ-16 and REQ-17.

02

Where the trail comes from

The event log is not a separate fixture sitting beside the dashboards. It is derived from the same two case pools every other tab already reads, which is why a case you drill into on the UM board has a trail that matches it.

03

What makes it tamper-evident

Each event hashes over its own content plus the hash of the event before it. Altering a record changes its hash, which changes the next record's input, and so on to the end of the log — so a single edit is visible from anywhere downstream of it.

04

Why the attribution fix mattered

SOD-3 asks whether a medical-necessity denial was issued by someone qualified to issue it. It answers that by reading the actor on the determination event — so who that event points at decides whether the control fires. The first build pointed it at the wrong person.

Still open

Appeals has no Audit & Compliance tab — the placeholder was removed rather than shown. It was three hardcoded percentages and three static flag rows, the state CM was in before this build. In front of an auditor that is worse than no tab at all, so it is gone from the sub-tab bar and its static rows deleted.

Building it for real is a bigger job than the CM rebuild was, because Appeals has no generated case pool — it runs on eight hand-authored appeal records and five reviewers, so there is nothing to sample, aggregate or drill into. The pool comes first: decided appeals across levels and lines of business, with acknowledgment and resolution clocks, reviewer credential and independence, and notice-content elements. The tab then follows the CM pattern.

Nothing else in Appeals changed — its eleven other tabs are untouched.
